# Topic 10: Cross-Functional Collaboration

## Overview
Working effectively with plant breeders, agronomists, field research teams, and regulatory affairs as an agricultural biotech AI specialist.

---

### Q1: How do you build effective working relationships with experienced plant breeders who may have decades of successful phenotypic-selection-based breeding experience and healthy skepticism toward genomic prediction/AI-driven approaches?

**A:**
**Trust-building approach:**
1. **Recognize and genuinely respect the real value of breeders' accumulated tacit knowledge, rather than positioning AI/genomic tools as a replacement for breeder judgment:** Experienced breeders often possess substantial, hard-won intuition about trait relationships, field performance patterns, and germplasm characteristics that isn't fully captured in any formal dataset — framing genomic prediction and computational tools as augmenting and informing breeder decision-making (providing an additional, valuable information source) rather than as a replacement for breeder expertise and judgment is both more accurate and more likely to be well-received
2. **Demonstrate genomic prediction's value through rigorous, transparent validation using metrics and comparisons breeders find genuinely convincing, not just statistical abstractions:** Presenting validation results in terms of realized selection outcomes (e.g., "genomic-selection-advanced lines from this cycle showed X% higher average performance than lines selected via phenotypic screening alone in a masked comparison") is typically more persuasive to experienced breeders than abstract statistical accuracy metrics alone — this requires the AI specialist to actively design validation studies framed around genuinely convincing, breeding-outcome-relevant comparisons, connecting to Topic 08's validation principles
3. **Be honest and specific about genomic prediction's current limitations, not just its capabilities:** Breeders who've encountered overconfident claims about genomic selection's capabilities (particularly during the technology's earlier hype cycles) are reasonably calibrated to be skeptical of similarly overconfident claims — being specific and honest about where genomic prediction currently adds clear value (e.g., early-generation screening efficiency) versus where phenotypic selection or breeder judgment remains essential (e.g., traits with currently poor genomic prediction accuracy, or entirely novel germplasm with limited genomic prediction training data relevance) builds more durable credibility
4. **Actively solicit and incorporate breeder domain knowledge into model design, not just model outputs into breeder decisions:** Involving breeders in discussions about which traits/environments should be prioritized for genomic prediction model development, and incorporating their knowledge about known trait relationships or germplasm characteristics into model design and interpretation, creates a genuinely collaborative relationship rather than a one-way "AI tells breeders what to do" dynamic that's both less likely to produce good outcomes and less likely to be well-received

### Q2: Describe how you would collaborate with field research/agronomy teams to ensure computational models are built on reliable, well-understood field trial data, and that model outputs translate into genuinely actionable field research recommendations.

**A:**
**Collaborative practices:**
1. **Engage directly with field data collection processes and their practical constraints, not just consume the resulting dataset in isolation:** Understanding firsthand (or through close consultation) the practical realities of field data collection — e.g., which phenotyping measurements are prone to particular sources of measurement error or inconsistency, what environmental/logistical challenges affected a specific season's data quality — provides essential context for appropriately interpreting and modeling the resulting data, and is difficult to fully substitute with documentation alone; the AI specialist should build genuine working relationships with field research teams rather than treating field data purely as an already-abstracted, context-free input
2. **Communicate data quality/completeness needs clearly and specifically, connecting to concrete modeling value, not as generic data requests:** Similar to the experimental design collaboration principles discussed in Topic 07, requests for improved data collection practices (e.g., more precise plot-level geolocation, more consistent phenotyping protocols) are more likely to be prioritized and well-executed by field teams when the AI specialist can articulate specifically how the improvement will translate into concrete modeling/decision-making value, rather than making abstract data-quality requests without clear connection to actual downstream use
3. **Translate model outputs into recommendations that respect field research teams' operational constraints and expertise:** A computational trial design recommendation (e.g., an optimal experimental design or environment selection strategy, Topic 07) that doesn't account for genuine field research logistical constraints (available land, seasonal timing, equipment/labor capacity) provides limited practical value regardless of its theoretical statistical optimality — the AI specialist should genuinely incorporate these practical constraints into recommendations, ideally through direct, iterative collaboration with field research team members who understand these constraints firsthand, rather than treating them as an afterthought to be negotiated after presenting a theoretically-optimal but impractical recommendation
4. **Close the loop by sharing modeling insights back to field teams, not just extracting data from them:** Sharing relevant modeling insights (e.g., "our analysis suggests this specific trial location has unusually high year-to-year variability, which may be worth investigating") back to field research teams, rather than a purely one-directional data-extraction relationship, both provides genuine value to field teams and reinforces a collaborative rather than extractive working relationship

## Summary
Effective cross-functional collaboration requires the agricultural biotech AI specialist to genuinely respect and integrate breeders' and agronomists' domain expertise and practical field constraints, communicate honestly about model capabilities and limitations, and build collaborative rather than purely extractive relationships with the field research and breeding teams whose work generates the data underlying all downstream computational modeling.
